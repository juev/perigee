# Low-vision reading: source notes

Ledger of the publications and documents read for issue [#144](https://github.com/juev/perigee/issues/144) and cited in [low-vision-reading.md](low-vision-reading.md). All were accessed on 2026-11-02. Each descriptive ID is the stable anchor linked from the review’s numeric footnotes.

How to read this ledger:

- **Read** means the page or file itself was opened and the passages named under *Access* were read. Search-result snippets were not used as evidence.
- Quotes are copied from the reading notes made when each source was opened. They are verbatim, including the source's own typography.
- *Date* is given only where the opened page showed it. Otherwise the entry says "no verified date".
- 28 distinct works are listed. One, `spacing2021`, was read as an abstract only. Alternative copies of one work (Cochrane's own summary page and PubMed Central; Legge 2016 in PMC, PubMed and the journal's landing page) are folded into one entry.
- [low-vision-candidates.csv](low-vision-candidates.csv) lists URLs found while searching. It is a discovery log, not a list of validated or independent studies, and most of its entries were not read.

## Clinical and psychophysical studies

### cochrane2018

- **Title:** Reading aids for adults with low vision
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494537 (also read: https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003303_reading-aids-adults-low-vision)
- **Date:** Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2018-04-17, CD003303.pub4, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD003303.pub4; search to 2018-01-17.
- **Type, population, task:** systematic review with GRADE certainty per outcome; 13 trials, 11 crossover (435 participants), one two-arm (37), one three-arm (243); mostly older adults with AMD; the main outcome is reading speed.
- **Quotes:**
  > There was moderate‐certainty evidence that reading duration was longer with the electronic devices and that they were easier to use.

  > We have not included studies investigating reading aids that allow reading through hearing, such as talking books or screen readers, or through touch, such as Braille-based devices and we did not consider rehabilitation strategies or complex low-vision interventions.
- **Limits:** certainty is low or moderate depending on the outcome. The tablet versus stand-mounted CCTV comparison (one study, 100 participants) found no important difference in speed, with imprecise estimates; that is not equivalence. Devices predate current phones. Audio aids are excluded by design, so the review says nothing for or against TTS. No study reported adverse outcomes.
- **Access:** PMC full text, relevant passages (abstract, outcome methods, conclusions), not every table; the Cochrane summary page with the full abstract.

### display2022

- **Title:** Technical Report: Digital Reading with Low Vision: Principles for Selecting Display Size
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9357187
- **Date:** final edited form 2022-06-23; Optom Vis Sci 99(8):655–661, August 2022; DOI 10.1097/OPX.0000000000001919.
- **Type:** narrative technical report with a selection procedure; no new trial.
- **Quotes:**
  > It should be noted that critical print size estimated from acuity may deviate significantly from the individual’s true critical print size, therefore this approach can only provide a rough estimation.

  > Note that font has a negligible impact on minimum display width due to the fortuitous trade-off that wider fonts have smaller critical print size but also take more space.
- **Limits:** when it summarises display2020 it gives 9 characters per line for low vision, where display2020 itself says 8, and it adopts 13 for its own procedure as the more inclusive value. Its acuity-reserve figures are rough rules, and the authors prefer measuring CPS directly.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, interaction of factors, obtaining CPS, minimum display width).

### display2020

- **Title:** Reconciling print-size and display-size constraints on reading
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7720185
- **Date:** PNAS 2020-11-09, 117(48):30276–30284; DOI 10.1073/pnas.2007514117.
- **Type, population, task:** psychophysics; 30 normally sighted readers, 10 readers with low vision of mixed diagnoses, and blur conditions; English text in Times and Courier, three display widths; one-minute trials.
- **Quotes:**
  > Our results showed that a minimum number of characters per line is required to achieve a criterion of 80% of maximum reading speed: 13 characters for normally sighted and eight characters for low-vision readers.

  > Each subject was asked 15 comprehension questions across 48 stories. No subject made more than three mistakes in the comprehension questions.
- **Limits:** a lower bound for 80 % of maximum speed in this experiment, not an ideal or recommended line length. Low-vision participants were chosen for being able and willing to read large print. Comprehension questions checked attention, not depth of understanding; no fatigue outcome.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, results, discussion, participants, procedure).

### acuity2018

- **Title:** Reading Acuity as a Predictor of Low-Vision Reading Performance
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6181187
- **Date:** IOVS, October 2018 (month only shown), 59(12):4798–4803; DOI 10.1167/iovs.18-24716.
- **Type, population, task:** observational psychophysics; 58 readers with low vision (30 macular, 28 non-macular) and 66 controls.
- **Quotes:**
  > RA is a better predictor than LA for spot reading size, fluent reading size, and critical print size.

  > All subjects were native English speakers with no known nonvisual reading disabilities.
- **Limits:** CPS here is the size for 90 % of maximum speed; "fluent" uses an 80 wpm criterion, not a validated comfort threshold. English only.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, subjects, procedure, analysis, acuity reserves, discussion).

### legge2016

- **Title:** Reading Digital with Low Vision
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5726769 (alternatives read: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29242668/, https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5926)
- **Date:** Visible Language, August 2016, 50(2):102–125; in PMC 2017-12-12.
- **Type:** narrative review by Gordon E. Legge.
- **Quotes:**
  > But a subset of people with low vision read 10% to 50% faster with bright letters on a black background.

  > Overall, the evidence indicates that increasing spacing between letters is not helpful, but extra-wide spacing between lines or words may have some benefits for some readers with low vision.
- **Limits:** summarises earlier studies; hardware it mentions is old. The spacing summary predates the RA/MRS distinction drawn in font2018 and spacing2021 and should be read with them.
- **Access:** full text in PMC, relevant sections (definitions; print size, spacing, font style, contrast and lighting); the two alternatives are abstract pages of the same work.

### crowding2017

- **Title:** Linking crowding, visual span, and reading
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5853154
- **Date:** Journal of Vision 2017-09-28, 17(11):11; DOI 10.1167/17.11.11.
- **Type, population, task:** mechanistic training study; 27 students with normal or corrected vision, letters 10° below fixation.
- **Quote:**
  > Reducing the impact of crowding can enlarge the visual span and can potentially facilitate reading, but not when adverse attentional bias is present.
- **Limits:** indirect evidence; no patients and no clinical efficacy.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, introduction, methods, first results).

### font2026

- **Title:** Assessment of newly designed fonts for visual accessibility
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13012447
- **Date:** PLOS ONE 2026-03-24, 21(3):e0345068; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0345068.
- **Type, population, task:** 22 young normally sighted adults (one excluded), reading with normal and filtered images that simulate reduced acuity.
- **Quotes:**
  > A broader definition of accessibility would include comprehension of the meaning of text as well, but comprehension is not included in the present analysis.

  > There was no significant difference in MRS across fonts (F(4, 180) = 0.80, p = 0.99).
- **Limits:** simulated, not actual, low vision; no comprehension; English; experiment 1 funded through Circle K, noted as a possible interest.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, introduction, experiment 1, funding).

### font2018

- **Title:** Fonts Designed for Macular Degeneration: Impact on Reading
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6100668
- **Date:** IOVS, August 2018 (month only shown), 59(10):4182–4189; DOI 10.1167/iovs.18-24334.
- **Type, population, task:** psychophysics; 19 readers with macular degeneration, 40 controls; digital MNREAD sentences.
- **Quotes:**
  > However, the two new fonts presented no advantage over Courier.

  > This means that the fonts that were most legible for the tiniest print were read more slowly for big print.
- **Limits:** CPS here is 95 % of maximum speed; short sentences at high contrast; English only.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages (abstract, subjects, fonts, methods, results, discussion).

### spacing2021

- **Title:** Increased letter spacing and greater letter width improve reading acuity in low vision readers
- **URL:** https://adk.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/increased-letter-spacing-and-greater-letter-width-improve-reading
- **Date:** Information Design Journal, 2021; DOI 10.1075/idj.19033.bei. No verified day.
- **Type, population, task:** 20 readers with AMD, Radner charts (from the abstract).
- **Quote:**
  > The results demonstrated a small, but measurable effect of letter spacing and letter width on reading acuity near critical font sizes.
- **Limits:** **abstract only**; the PDF returned 404. No effect sizes, optimal values or long-form outcomes can be taken from it.
- **Access:** institutional abstract only.

## Interaction and reading-mode studies

### scrolling2021

- **Title:** A comparison of reading, in people with simulated and actual central vision loss, with static text, horizontally scrolling text, and rapid serial visual presentation
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8590178
- **Date:** Journal of Vision 2021-11-09, 21(12):5; DOI 10.1167/jov.21.12.5.
- **Type, population, task:** crossover; study 2 had 37 people with macular degeneration (mean age 77.54) reading short texts aloud at home, then answering questions; study 1 had 15 students with a simulated scotoma.
- **Quotes:**
  > Total comprehension (Figure 10c) was better with the scrolling text display (mean = 74.15%, SE = 3.33) compared with all other text display types (RSVP mean = 46.22%, SE = 3.81; single-line mean = 50.66%, SE = 4.05; multiple-line paragraph format mean = 58.31%, SE = 3.65).

  > Rereading is not possible with RSVP, and the inability to go back over text to reinspect it may result in the reader prioritizing single-word processing with a diminished level of integration between words (Schotter et al., 2014).
- **Limits:** "scrolling" means one line moving horizontally, not vertical scrolling of a page. Static text was read faster (104.72 against 71.90 wpm for the moving line). Short passages read aloud; RSVP ran on a different tablet; print was twice each person's CPS; no eye tracking in patients.
- **Access:** full text.

### effect2023

- **Title:** Eccentric Viewing Training for Age-Related Macular Disease: Results of a Randomized Controlled Trial (the EFFECT Study)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767206
- **Date:** published online 2023-10-31; Ophthalmology Science 4(2):100422 (2024 issue); DOI 10.1016/j.xops.2023.100422.
- **Type, population, task:** RCT, 200 adults with AMD, four arms, three 45-minute sessions plus home practice, six-month follow-up.
- **Quotes:**
  > There was no difference between groups on change in task ability (F(3,174) = 1.48, P = 0.22) or on any of the secondary outcome measures.

  > Our results do not support the routine use of eccentric viewing training for people with progressing age-related macular disease, although this training may help people with end-stage disease.
- **Limits:** one fixed protocol and dose; wide severity range. It does not show that all rehabilitation is useless.
- **Access:** full text.

### gazeprompt2024

- **Title:** GazePrompt: Enhancing Low Vision People’s Reading Experience with Gaze-Aware Augmentations
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12772v2
- **Date:** arXiv:2402.12772v2, 2024-02-23 (first submitted 2024-02-20). Preprint.
- **Type, population, task:** lab study; 13 participants with low vision reading aloud on a desktop screen with an eye tracker; a second, silent-reading study of 13 (23 unique overall).
- **Quotes:**
  > While there was no significant improvement on line switching accuracy (H1.2), GazePrompt enabled more flexible scrolling behaviors, not requiring low vision users to scroll only one or two lines at a time to reduce line switching difficulty.

  > One participant felt the background color change caused by Line Highlighting during line switching distracted them, which negatively affected their reading comprehension (P22).
- **Limits:** line-switching time fell (p = .014), but accuracy and misread words did not change significantly. Single sessions; gaze-driven, so it says nothing about a manual ruler or a phone camera.
- **Access:** full HTML of v2.

## Font studies outside low vision

### bionic2025

- **Title:** Guiding the Gaze: How Bionic Reading Influences Eye Movements
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662
- **Date:** 2025-10-01, 18(5):49; DOI 10.3390/jemr18050049 (as shown in PMC; journal name not captured in the extract).
- **Type, population, task:** eye-tracking study; 53 adults, convenience sample, two passages, standard against Bionic font.
- **Quotes:**
  > Fixation durations, number of fixations and reading speeds were not significantly different between the two formats.

  > All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.
- **Limits:** not low vision; short-term; a null result here does not prove that no reader can prefer or benefit from the format.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages.

### dyslexie2017

- **Title:** Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5934461
- **Date:** 2017-12-04, 68(1):25–42; DOI 10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6 (as shown in PMC).
- **Type, population, task:** two experiments with children, 170 with dyslexia in the first; 102 with and 45 without dyslexia in the second; Dyslexie against Arial and Times New Roman.
- **Quotes:**
  > In Experiment 1, children with dyslexia (n = 170) did not read text written in Dyslexie font faster or more accurately than in Arial font.

  > The majority preferred reading in Arial and preference was not related to reading performance.
- **Limits:** children and dyslexia, not low vision; one typeface.
- **Access:** full text, relevant passages.
